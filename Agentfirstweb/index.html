The Transformation

The web is evolving from human-readable pages to agent-accessible data services

The coming agent-first web will reshape how information and services are accessed. Rather than humans clicking links and reading pages, AI assistants ("agents") will query data sources and take actions on users' behalf. This means websites may no longer be primarily human-readable pages; instead, they'll expose structured data, APIs, or "agent-optimized" interfaces.

Google's AI lead Demis Hassabis envisions a web as "a series of databases" for agents to query, not pages to render. In practice, analysts expect a hybrid phase where sites maintain dual interfaces – one for humans and a simplified, semantically rich "agent view" that uses enhanced metadata and clear ARIA labels.

Sites like Amazon or travel platforms might offer explicit tools (e.g. "search availability") with clear input/output specs, rather than hiding all logic in a GUI. In short, web content will shift from static pages to "actionable" data services: agents learn available capabilities, pick tools, and complete user goals autonomously.

Service Industries: Embracing or Evading Agents

Consumer services – food delivery, e-commerce, booking – face both promise and peril. If agents aggregate offerings, individual providers could be abstracted away. Why would a restaurant or courier want to be just an API in an agent's mix?

Sundar Pichai addresses this: it's akin to dine-in vs. delivery. A restaurant can ignore takeout, or go "all in" on delivery and virtual ordering. Similarly, some businesses may refuse to enable agent-access (focusing on their own customer apps), while others lean in as a new sales channel.

Dara Khosrowshahi (Uber) suggests companies will "do it because it's cool" and test if it adds value – and if so, "charge a big fee." In effect, agents become another distribution channel: one that might boost volume but threatens margins if unregulated.

🍽️

Food Delivery & Restaurants

Agents query menus across providers and handle automatic booking/delivery. Opportunities: Reach more customers via AI, increased order volume. Risks: Margin squeeze from aggregator fees, loss of brand presentation.

🛒

E-commerce & Retail

Agents compare products across sites and enable auto-checkout at lowest prices. Opportunities: Product discovery by new users, cross-selling via recommendations. Risks: Intense price competition, possible "middleman" fees.

✈️

Travel & Booking

Agents optimize itineraries and book through APIs with best deals. Opportunities: Access to instant dynamic pricing, package bundling. Risks: Disintermediation, dependency on travel platform deals.

🚗

Transportation

Agents plan multi-leg trips and book rides across apps (ride + train). Opportunities: Seamless mobility, loyalty bundling. Risks: Regulatory concerns, commission battles if multiple services integrated.

🛍️

Rapid Delivery

Agents allocate tasks to nearest provider, optimize routes, order from multiple stores. Opportunities: Efficiency gains, 24/7 ordering, dynamic bundling. Risks: Competition on margin, data privacy, integration complexity.

New Business Models Will Emerge:

💰 Commissions or Fees:

Platforms may charge transaction fees when an agent triggers a sale, similar to Expedia or app stores.

🏆 Certified Integrations:

Official "agent APIs" or ChatGPT plugins for ordering. Early movers may capture prominent placement.

📊 Data-sharing Incentives:

Grant agents access in exchange for improved recommendations or revenue-sharing.

🎯 Co-branding & Loyalty:

Offer discounts or loyalty points for orders placed through agents to drive adoption.

⚠️ Critical Insight

While agents can erode direct customer engagement, non-participation risks obsolescence. If an agent can't find you, it will pick a competitor. The balance between maintaining brand experience and tapping AI-driven demand will be a key strategic challenge.

🌏 Asia's Advantage

In Asia, super-apps (WeChat, Gojek, Grab) already integrate many services via mini-programs – a natural base for agent integration. Asian economies' fast AI adoption means businesses here are actively experimenting with "agent assistants" internally. However, fragmentation (language, regulation) could slow a uniform transition.

Media Publishers: Protecting Content and Revenue

Media and publishing face acute disruption. If AI agents answer user queries by summarizing articles, click-driven traffic will fall. Google's "AI Overviews" already show summaries atop search results; one study found publishers can lose 15–60% of organic clicks on AI-prioritized queries.

Worse, when agents like ChatGPT or Bing summarize news, they may not faithfully cite or link to sources. A Columbia Journalism study found ChatGPT often "invent[ed] or misrepresent[ed]" citations, hurting publishers' trust in agents. In practice, many AI answers provide no link ("direct" traffic), as seen when Copilot-directed visits register as "Direct" in analytics.

Publishers see this as a form of "contextual content theft." Current ad/subscription models rely on eyeballs landing on the publisher's page. Agents threaten this funnel.

💰

Licensing Deals

Major outlets are signing AI licensing agreements. OpenAI has paid publishers (e.g. Axel Springer, FT, NewsCorp) millions annually for access to recent content, creating new revenue streams and ensuring proper integration.

🔌

AI-Friendly APIs

Publishers offer paid APIs for article summaries with micropayment models (per-article or per-query fees), allowing agents to "check out" content rather than copy for free. A proposed concept is "AI micropayments."

🛡️

Restrictive Licensing/Gating

To prevent "free riding," sites may robot-block non-licensed crawlers or serve partial content. However, overzealous blocking risks being bypassed by visual agents or harming SEO.

📱

Value-add in Platforms

Publishers embed their own AI widgets or branded agents that deliver content value while controlling interface and ad placement. CNN or FT could build official ChatGPT plugins charging for full-text access.

⚖️

Quality & Attribution Enforcement

Pressuring AI companies through copyright claims and regulation to enforce source citation. The lack of guaranteed attribution in AI answers is a legal and reputational risk.

Publisher Strategy Evolution Timeline

📈 Discovery & Traffic - TODAY
Current State: Publishers rely on search rankings and social links for traffic discovery.

Agent Era Challenge: AI answers replace many search clicks, reducing direct traffic.

Response Strategy: License AI distribution for payment; develop new AI search SEO (AEO) practices.
💰 Monetization Evolution
Current State: Revenue from ad views, paywalls, and subscriptions based on page visits.

Agent Era Challenge: Ad impressions drop as bots reuse content freely without visits.

Response Strategy: Negotiate licensing fees from AI firms; create premium content API access models.
⚖️ Content Use & Protection
Current State: Intellectual property protected by traditional copyright frameworks.

Agent Era Challenge: AI summarization may infringe copyright or dilute brand value.

Response Strategy: Demand proper citations; implement watermark/metadata tagging; pursue legal actions.
👥 User Behavior - FUTURE
Current State: Users click through to articles and engage with advertising content.

Agent Era Challenge: Users get answers within AI interfaces, bypassing publisher sites.

Response Strategy: Create engagement incentives; develop interactive content; build branded AI assistants.

📉 The Challenge

Even small license fees are "peanuts" to AI giants but keep lawsuits at bay. Some agencies argue "AI Overviews" may dilute publisher authority, effectively acting like paywall circumvention under a new name.

🔮 The Future

The future likely blends licensing with new AI-centric ad or subscription models. The industry is calling for "fair use" norms or legislation to ensure content creators aren't left unpaid by AI disintermediation.

⚠️ Bottom Line

Without changes, publishers risk losing a large share of their referral traffic and ad revenues. All parties are still experimenting with licensing, attribution requirements, and new AI-centric business models.

Agentic Business Models: Enterprises, Platforms, and Users

Enterprises and Platforms see agents as opportunities to create new products and efficiencies. Internally, companies deploy AI agents as digital assistants: marketing assistants that craft copy, sales bots that sift CRM data, or support agents that handle IT tickets.

The Microsoft APAC survey highlights that over half of Asian companies already use agents to fully automate processes. "Frontier firms" with human-agent hybrid teams report higher agility and growth.

🏢

Enterprise SaaS & AI Agents as a Service

Vendors (OpenAI, Google, Amazon, local Asian players like Baidu/Tencent) offer agent-building tools. Companies build custom assistants for HR, finance, R&D and subscribe to AI platforms. Some sell "vertical agents" for specific industries.

🏪

Platform Marketplaces

Analogous to app stores, platforms host marketplaces for agent skills. OpenAI's GPT Store and upcoming "developer revenue program" show this model: creators build specialized GPTs and share revenue from usage.

👤

User Subscription Models

Premium agent subscriptions for end-users. Just as many pay for ChatGPT Plus or Microsoft Copilot, future agents with unique domain knowledge could command fees (financial advisor agents, travel planning agents).

🤝

Commissions/Affiliates

Agents connecting consumers to services earn referral fees. Brands might pay platforms to be featured or power branded agents. An airline might pay to integrate booking systems for upselling flights.

📊

Data and Insights

Agents generate wealth of user intent data. Platforms may monetize anonymized insights or target ads more precisely through agent interactions. However, privacy and trust will be major concerns.

Consumer Experience Transformation

Consumers will experience the web largely through agents or AI-enhanced interfaces. Instead of manually comparing products, a consumer might "ask my assistant" to handle planning or shopping.

In Asia, where messaging and super-apps are prevalent, users may trust AI assistants provided by familiar platforms (WeChat or Line integrating agents). A common model: basic agent usage is free, but "pro" features (complex tasks, real-time help) require premium tiers.

🌏 Asia's Strategic Advantage

Asia's strong AI adoption suggests these models will emerge quickly. A majority of APAC leaders see agents as collaborators (not just tools). Asian tech giants are already adding AI into messaging and e-commerce platforms, with consumers accustomed to "chat-based shopping."

Industry Signals: OpenAI, Google I/O and the Road Ahead

Recent AI releases illustrate the agent-first trend. The transition has already begun with concrete product announcements that confirm agents will consume and act on web content rather than just retrieve it.

These developments underline key trends: multimodal, tool-enabled models (GPT-4o and Google's Gemini 2.5) are being tied to action tools (browsing, API calls, even controlling apps). The emerging norm is that LLMs won't just answer queries, but will be granted capabilities to do things.

🚀

OpenAI's Platform Evolution

March 2025: Agents SDK and Responses API introduced as building blocks for custom agents. Includes built-in tools (web search, file search, "computer use") and observability, explicitly targeting autonomous task completion.

🤖

Operator: Computer-Use Agents

January 2025: OpenAI released Operator, an agent that can open a browser and interact like a user (filling forms, clicking buttons, even ordering groceries). Demonstrates what "computer-use" agents can accomplish.

🔍

Google I/O 2025 Announcements

"Agent Mode" in Search allows conversational workflows. "Deep Search" for iterative queries. New agentic plugins for tasks like ticket booking (Project Mariner). Agent Mode in Gemini app lets users state goals and have AI enact multi-step tasks.

🌏

Global AI Race

Asian giants (Alibaba's "Tongyi Qianwen", Baidu's "Ernie Bot X") aim for consumer app integration as early agents. Unlike Western markets, many Asian consumers are accustomed to "chat-based shopping," smoothing the path for agent commerce.

⚡

Multimodal Tool Integration

GPT-4o variants now offer web-search backends with high factual accuracy. Google's Gemini-2.5 will power enterprise agents via Vertex AI. This "AI as an assistant with agency" represents a fundamental shift in capabilities.

📈

Early Market Signals

Agent "shopping assistants" and "ticket-booking agents" are already baked into search. These early implementations serve as proof-of-concept for more sophisticated agent ecosystems to come.

📊 What This Means for Product Managers

Agents are quickly moving from hype to product reality. Whether it's generative chat with browsing (ChatGPT with Operator), or personalized search (Google AI Mode), user interactions are shifting. Early adopters that define agent interfaces, APIs, and business models will shape consumer expectations.

🌐 The Global Perspective

US tech leaders (OpenAI, Google, Microsoft) and Asian giants (Tencent, Alibaba, Samsung) are racing to integrate agents. Policies and standards will emerge (e.g. the need for "Agent-Engine Optimization" akin to SEO).

🔮 The Future is Agent-First

The web is poised to evolve into a mesh of agent-centric data endpoints. Service businesses must decide how to coexist with AI intermediaries; media must protect value in an era of AI digests; enterprises must leverage agents internally and externally.

The companies that invest now in structured APIs, AI-agent partnerships, and innovative business models will lead the agent-first future.
