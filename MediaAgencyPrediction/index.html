The Future and Fate of Media Agencies

Will Legacy Giants Survive the age of AI and digital Transformation? An in-depth analysis of the traditional ad agency model under siege

Executive Summary

The traditional ad agency model – based on media commissions and siloed services – is under siege. Holding companies show mixed performance: WPP and IPG have reported declines, while Publicis and Omnicom grew modestly. A GroupM forecast predicts global ad spend will surpass $1 trillion in 2024, with 72–78% going to digital platforms like Google, Meta and ByteDance, signalling that agencies must compete with tech giants for value.

Internally, legacy networks suffer high turnover and burnout: surveys report ~56% of creatives intend to quit within a year and nearly half of holding-company staff feel job insecurity. In response, some employees have launched nimble independent agencies with startup-like equity models. Meanwhile, consultancies and big tech are encroaching on agency turf by offering in-house media buying and AI-driven creative tools.

Our analysis finds that legacy agencies will only survive the next 5–7 years if they radically transform: digitize services, adopt outcome-based fees, invest in talent and tech. Otherwise, many will cede ground to specialized boutiques, in-house teams, or tech platforms.

The Traditional Model Under Siege

For over a century, agencies earned standard commissions on media buys, but that model is rapidly eroding. Brands now demand transparency and performance-based fees that tie agency compensation directly to business outcomes.

A World Federation of Advertisers survey found 75% of multinationals want to change agency remuneration away from commissions toward outcome-based contracts. Non-transparent practices like undeclared media rebates have come under scrutiny – a 2016 ANA report warned agencies must provide "full disclosure" of all rebates or lose client trust.

Meanwhile, major agencies are scrambling to cut costs. WPP's CEO recently announced layoffs in its media arm (GroupM) as part of a global restructure, and Omnicom cut ~3,000 jobs in 2024 to align with its planned IPG merger. These moves reflect a shift to leaner operations but also underscore the pressure on agencies' old hierarchies and billable-hour models.

Financial and Market Trends

The ad industry overall is still growing – GroupM projects $1.04 trillion in global ad spend for 2024 (up ~9.5%), reaching $1.1T by 2025. But nearly 73% of that will go to digital platforms by 2025.

Google, Meta, ByteDance (TikTok) and Amazon/Alibaba now command over half of all ad dollars. This consolidation of demand makes it harder for agencies to differentiate; clients can buy "ads + AI targeting" directly from platforms.

Within the holding groups, fortunes vary significantly. Yet even amid growth pockets, profits are squeezed. Omnicom set a target to cut its staff compensation bill 10% ahead of the IPG deal. Publicly, holding company leaders are reorganizing portfolios – WPP and IPG are merging agency brands, GroupM is consolidating Mindshare/Essence/Wavemaker into one P&L – essentially retrenching the legacy structure.

Talent Crisis and Culture Clash

A critical weakness of big agencies is their workforce. Multiple studies report alarmingly low morale and high churn. In a survey of ~1,000 creatives, 56% said they planned to leave their current job within a year, and 75% described themselves as "meh-to-miserable" at work.

Similarly, Adweek data shows U.S. agency headcount fell ~4% since April 2023 even as the overall job market grew, reflecting layoffs and departures. Holding-company employees are especially anxious: an Adweek poll found 49% feel insecure about their jobs and "nearly half" are actively looking to quit agency life.

The work environment is often cited as toxic: long hours, low pay increases and whitewashed "burnout days" contribute to overwork. Many experienced staff hedge their bets by freelancing or moonlighting – one survey found 65% of agency creatives were working a side gig, usually undisclosed – and some secretly build startups or gig incomes.

Competition from Independents and In-House Teams

All this turnover is feeding the rise of nimble independents. Adweek reports "industry veterans are leaving big agencies to build indie shops with fresh, bold approaches." These new boutiques eschew holdco bureaucracy: they are faster, flatter, and often tech-savvy.

Campaign Asia profiles Indian independents, noting they have "ditched old-school business practices" and "are embracing tech, AI, and holistic strategies to future-proof their companies." In their view, the "traditional agency model… is rapidly fading" and independents' agility makes them thrive in today's landscape.

Case Study: Talented (India)

A striking case is Talented (India), founded in 2022 by former Dentsu Webchutney leaders. They explicitly aim to "keep people at the core…take a startup approach, and focus on being a pure-creative agency."

Talented's founders tout employee equity: their business model includes giving ~20% of the company to staff via ESOPs. In their words, "the world…doesn't need another advertising agency. What it does need is a serious re-imagination of the agency experience…Working in advertising can and must feel better."

Talented also rejects excessive hierarchy ("no follow-ups," flat structure) to enable creativity. These indie agencies are winning accounts by promising faster turnaround and founder-level attention – and major brands are noticing.

Meanwhile, many big brands are partially in-housing their marketing. Global companies (from Unilever to HP) have built internal creative/media teams, using agencies only for specialized projects. This trend further shrinks legacy networks' roles. The net effect: mid-market brands in particular find indie shops and in-housed teams more appealing and cost-effective.

Technology and Consultancy Threats

Legacy agencies also lag in tech. Clients' media dollars are increasingly spent through automated programmatic platforms. Major consultancies (Accenture Interactive, Deloitte Digital, IBM iX, PwC Digital) have built or acquired large ad units to capture this business.

Accenture Interactive's revenue rose 35% to $6.5B, aided by acquiring 14 agencies in five years. Deloitte bought creative shop Heat (LG and Papa Murphy's cases). IBM and PwC have built hundreds of studios. The consulting pitch: agencies are selling the same "technology" (adtech, data analytics) that consultancies now offer, so advertisers might as well work with outfits that deliver end-to-end business strategy.

On the media-buying side, platforms are making DIY easier. Meta/Google now tout AI-driven creative tools and audience targeting. Mark Zuckerberg stated that AI will generate "better creative options" for advertisers while the platform handles optimal targeting.

Corporate Responsibility: PR vs. Progress

Legacy agencies often tout commitments to diversity, sustainability and social causes. However, critics argue these are largely PR exercises without structural change. For example, WPP – which had billed itself as a "diversity leader" – quietly removed all references to "diversity, equity and inclusion" from its 2024 annual report. (It now talks vaguely of "people and culture.")

This suggests undercurrent political and image pressures, rather than deep reform. Similarly, many agencies run token ESG campaigns and charity events, but their internal hierarchies and compensation practices remain unchanged. In short, big agencies have lots of glossy headlines (Cannes prizes, CSR initiatives) but still grapple with core cultural problems of pay, promotion and purpose.

The Verdict: Transform or Perish

The evidence points to a stark reality: without radical reinvention, many legacy agencies may not survive in their current form over the next 5–7 years. The old recipe – commission-based fees, siloed teams, emphasis on awards over client results – is breaking down.

Some legacy agencies will survive, but only by becoming unrecognizable from today's versions. We expect consolidation and closures as ongoing, but also opportunity: startups like Talented, specialist shops and tech-led firms will capture growing share.
